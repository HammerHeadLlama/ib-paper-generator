How the random picker works

Each question in the bank has a record: year, session, time zone, paper, question number, marks, which syllabus subtopics each part covers, which parts depend on earlier parts, whether it needs the data or formula booklet, and where the question and its mark scheme are on the original pages. The picker only uses these records. The question itself is cropped from your own PDFs at the end.

1. Which questions can be picked

A question can be picked when all of these are true:

  • its year is inside your year range, and it's from the current syllabus unless you allow older ones;
  • you loaded the paper it comes from (if you loaded a folder);
  • it isn't in a paper on your Exclusions list, and isn't already in this paper or removed from it;
  • enough of it is on your topics. With "at least half", a question qualifies when the parts tagged with your topics carry at least half of its marks. "Every part" is stricter, and "any part" is looser.

2. Weighting

Each question that can be picked gets a weight from your topic weights (A bit 0.3, Normal 1, A lot 2.5), averaged over its parts by marks. Questions then go into a random order where heavier ones tend to come first (weighted random sampling). The order differs every time, so questions mix freely across years.

3. Hitting the marks exactly

  • Multiple choice: each question is 1 mark, so the picker takes as many as you asked for.
  • Written papers: the picker walks down the random order and adds each whole question that still fits in the marks left.
  • It then fills the last few marks with stand-alone parts: parts of other questions that don't depend on earlier parts, for example Q4(c) on its own. A small search finds a combination of at most four parts whose marks add up exactly.
  • If no combination fits, it drops one or two of the last whole questions and tries again, up to 40 times. If the questions still can't reach your total, the review shows how many marks are missing.

4. Rounds

In the review you can remove or replace questions, or untick parts. Parts that a kept part depends on stay in automatically. Fill the gaps runs step 3 again only for the marks that are missing, never reusing a question you removed. Each round is still random, but reproducible.

5. Seeds and paper codes

The seed drives all the randomness: the same settings and the same seed always give the same first-round paper. A paper code is different: it records the exact final list of questions and kept parts, after any edits. Use the paper code to rebuild a paper or to exclude it later; a seed alone can't do that, because the same seed gives a different paper under different settings.

6. Building the PDFs

For each question, the page cuts the recorded rectangles out of your original PDFs and places them on A4 pages as vector graphics, so text stays sharp. The mark scheme is built the same way, in the same order. Multiple-choice answers come from the answer key.

Questions, mark schemes and diagrams come from your own copies of IB past papers. This tool only records where each question is and which syllabus topics it covers.
